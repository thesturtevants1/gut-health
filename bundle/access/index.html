The gut foundations

Gut is one of the 5 drivers of visceral fat, and one of the most commonly mistreated. This is the exact framework we use with our clients.

Part two · The connection

Why your gut and your weight are connected

If you've been gaining weight in menopause despite eating well and staying active, ask yourself this: did your weight gain actually start ON ITS OWN?

Around the same time, did you also notice
Your sleep getting worseFeeling more tired through the dayMore brain fog or mood changesFeeling bloated or sluggish after mealsChanges in your bowel movementsFeeling hungry or craving food

Your weight gain hasn't happened in isolation

Yet when women try to lose weight, they focus almost entirely on eating less, cutting foods or exercising more, and never stop to investigate what else has been changing in their body.

The overlooked piece

Your gut isn't only about bloating and constipation.

Your gut microbiome is involved in how your body processes nutrients, regulates appetite and supports metabolic health. And as estrogen changes in menopause, your microbiome can change too.

To be clear

This doesn't automatically mean your gut is causing your weight gain. But if you're struggling with your weight alongside fatigue, brain fog, mood changes or digestive issues, it's certainly worth paying attention to.

Think back: what else was changing when your weight started going up?

Where cutting back backfires

Cutting carbs often cuts fibre

Bread, oats and potatoes are also important sources of fibre. Fibre helps you feel fuller, supports appetite regulation and feeds the beneficial bacteria in your gut. When those bacteria ferment fibre they produce short-chain fatty acids, which are involved in signalling linked to appetite and blood sugar regulation. Less fibre can make hunger harder to manage, and can add to constipation, bloating and poor sleep.

“Some” protein isn’t enough protein

An egg at breakfast, a little chicken at lunch, some fish at dinner: protein at every meal, but a LOW intake. Protein helps preserve muscle, which affects how much energy you use. It helps with fullness, making a sustainable deficit easier. And your gut needs it: amino acids repair your intestinal lining, and your digestive enzymes are proteins too.

Eating less isn't the same as eating better. Sometimes the next step isn't removing more food, it's finding what your body is missing.

It's

"What is missing?"

Gut symptoms are rarely fixed by removing a single food or reaching for a probiotic. Across the thousands of women we've worked with, a probiotic has very rarely been the answer. In most cases we actually remove them.

Why this matters more in menopause

Around 40 to 45, every woman's gut microbiome shifts, and its diversity keeps declining after that. If you come into menopause with poor gut health already, that shift is quicker and harsher.

Then layer on top
High or dysregulated cortisol Poor sleep Less movement Years of dieting and skipped meals Undereating carbs, fibre and protein Too many supplements

Any one of these on top of the natural shift is where we see gut symptoms get much more severe.

Part two · 05

The 4 foundations

1. Regular meals

Eat at regular times through the day. Skipped meals are part of the problem.

2. 30g fibre a day

Fruit and veg with every meal. A variety, because different types of fibre feed different bacteria. Wholegrain and wholemeal versions. Soluble AND insoluble, spread evenly through your meals.

3. Water, at least 2 litres a day

Fibre needs water to do its job.

4. Sufficient protein, from real food

Processed protein (like shakes) is often the first thing we remove, because it's very hard to digest.

And carbohydrates go back in.

If you're not eating enough carbohydrates, you do not have a balanced diet, point blank.

We reintroduce them slowly, not all in one week.

Part two · 08

When we go more granular

Think of a broken machine. It makes a lot of noise. Cutting out foods is like switching the machine off: the noise stops, but the machine is still broken. That's why elimination diets give temporary relief and then nothing.

So we work in 3 phases

1 Screen and identify

Find the gasoline on the fire and stop it, whether that's undereating or certain trigger foods.

2 Strengthen the microbiome

Once the baseline has settled, we rebuild diversity through varied nutrition, not restriction: more plant variety, probiotic-rich foods (live yoghurt, kefir, sauerkraut, kimchi), and vitamin E foods (sunflower seeds, almonds, avocado).

3 External factors

Nervous system and cortisol regulation, so the gut stays strong for the long term.

Phase 2 · For bloating

Find trigger foods, remove them, reintroduce them gradually, and avoid fizzy drinks.

Phase 2 · For constipation

Prunes, kiwi with the skin on, and coffee in the morning.

The cost of long-term restriction

Cutting out gluten, dairy and carbs for years, or eating very little fibre, always reduces microbiome diversity. That's a FASTER decline than the natural menopausal one, with fewer digestive enzymes and more sensitivity over time.

Sue is menopausal and came to us for gut health and weight.

  • The scale had gone up for years, then got stuck.

Every time she saw a specialist, she was handed another medication, protocol, detox or elimination.

Nobody stepped back to ask WHY.

What the 7-day screening found

She was undereating the nutrients the gut needs to form and clear bile. That was the missing piece beneath the constipation and bloating, and it had never been picked up.

What we did
Week 1

We removed processed protein and restructured every meal so it had enough protein and fibre. We gave her a meal plan so we could see exactly what was working. Over the first 3 weeks we slowly reintroduced carbohydrates.

Week 2

We introduced meal spacing, set from her tracker, to support gut motility.

Did NOT

Add any supplements (we stopped her probiotics) or eliminate any more foods.

A bowel movement every 1 to 2 days, more formed and easier to pass.

5/5→2–3

The scale started moving after years of being stuck, with NO change to her overall intake or exercise. All we changed was her gut.

Where she is now

Phase 2. Her baseline has settled, so we're expanding her food variety to rebuild her microbiome.

Repair isn't always light on the scale

When we start fixing sleep, gut, cortisol, inflammation and blood sugar, your body is repairing. Inflammation shifts, fluid shifts, digestion changes, and your food intake goes up, not down. The scale can sit still, or even go up, while your body does exactly what we asked it to.

Your timeline depends on YOU

Not on an average. It's set by which symptoms you have, how severe they are, and how long they've been going on. The longer and more severe, the more of your first phase is repair work.

Measure month one with a scale, and you'll quit in month one.

What to measure in the first 30 days

Your root driver, because that's what is actually holding your weight on. If it's your gut, use the markers from Part two: bloating, bowel movements, stool consistency, discomfort after meals, brain fog and energy.

Weight loss in menopause is a byproduct of reducing the things driving the weight.
